\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Optogenetics uses light to control the activity of neurons by expression of light-sensitive proteins genetically expressed in cells of interest [REF]. 
The technique has been used very extensively in systems neuroscience to help dissect the relationship between neural activity and behaviour [REF review paper]. 
Since its invention, it has become the preferred experimental tool due to its high temporal precision and genetically-constrained impact of modulation [REF].

In small animals such as Drosophila [REF], where excellent genetic control is available, indiscriminately applied light affects only the chosen subpopulation of neurons expressing the transgene.
In vertebrates, the transgene is typically expressed in a cell \textit{type} of interest (\cite{Zhao2011CellFunction}), thus additional spatial specificity of the light delivery becomes necessary.
Spatially discriminant light delivery in behaving rodents was initially achieved via a chronically implanted fibre in a freely moving animal [REFs].
Whilst this approach allows the animal to move around its environment, only a small, fixed, number of stimulation sites in sufficiently distant configuration (due to the physical size of implants) is possible.
More recently, wireless implants housing a micro-LED have been used to deliver light in one location to freely moving mice (\cite{Ausra2021WirelessAnimals}) .

A more flexible approach becomes possible in head-fixed animals, since here the photostimulation location can easily be targeted by programmatically moving the light source during an experiment. 
Whilst this can be achieved with a fibre optic and motorized micro-drives (\cite{Zatka-Haas2021SensoryDecision}), a faster approach is to scan a focused laser beam over the exposed skull using galvanometric scan mirrors (galvos).
Galvos are composed of two mirrors mounted on servomotors and oriented orthogonally to one another. 
They are commonly used in confocal and multi-photon microscopy for raster-scanning a laser beam over the sample. 
Several studies have recently used galvos to position a focused photo-activation point onto the exposed skull (\cite{Guo2014FlowMice,Li2016RobustPlanning,Inagaki2018Low-dimensionalCortex, Keller2020FeedbackCortex,Voitov2022CorticalMemory,Pinto2019Task-DependentRegions,Coen2021FrontalModalities,Pinto2022MultipleCortex}).
For example, \cite{Guo2014FlowMice} used channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) to inhibit neural activity in order to study a whisker-based tactile task with a memory component. 
This approach has become increasingly popular due to its relatively low cost, speed, and potentially compact size. 

The last decade has seen widespread adoption of open source hardware tools in neuroscience, such as Open-Ephys (\cite{Siegle2017OpenElectrophysiology}), the `Pulse-Pal' pulse generator (\cite{Sanders2014ABehavior}), the `Stimjim` programmable electrical stimulator (\cite{Cermak2019Stimjim:Stimulation}), as well as countless designs for light-sheet and multi-photon microscopes. 
However, there exists no good open source solution for programmable photo-stimulation in head-fixed behaving mice.
We hope `Zapit', presented in this paper, fills this gap.

‘Zapit’ is the combination of an open-source galvo-based hardware design and an associated
software ecosystem for calibration and stimulus delivery. 
The hardware was designed with the intention of making it as compact as possible so it can fit into tight behavioural enclosures. 
Relative low cost and short lead times ensure it remains accessible.
Our system is flexible regarding inactivation protocols, from single points to multiple areas targeted bilaterally.
We demonstrate how Zapit allows for fast and easy integration of targeted photostimulation into a variety of behavioural tasks.
