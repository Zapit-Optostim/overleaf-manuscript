\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Optogenetics has become the preferred experimental tool for manipulating neural activity in systems neuroscience due to its high temporal precision, reversibility, and both genetically and spatially restricted impact of modulation (\cite{Boyden2005Millisecond-timescaleActivity,OConnor2009ReverseBrain,Li2019SpatiotemporalCircuits,Emiliani2022OptogeneticsSystems.}).
Light delivery for the activation of optogenetic actuators is predominantly achieved through chronically implanted fibers (e.g. \cite{Mayrhofer2019DistinctTransformation,Babl2019TheInterneurons,Lohse2021SubcorticalMice,Duan2021ATask,Emiliani2022OptogeneticsSystems.}), or by implanting a window over the cortical site (e.g. \cite{Schneider2018AMovement,Liu2016Cortico-fugalBehaviour}).
Although fiber optics allow for manipulation of neural activity in both head-fixed and freely moving behavioral paradigms, the physical footprint and chronic nature of implants restrict manipulation to a predetermined and small number of sites. 
 
In mice, the skull is thin enough ( $\sim$250~$\mu m$; \cite{Ghanbari2019Craniobot:Microsurgeries}) that light can pass through it, and it is feasible to image the surface of the dorsal brain through the intact skull of a head-fixed animal (\cite{Steinzeig2017ChronicMice,Guo2014FlowMice}). This transcranial optical access allows for flexible, random access optogenetic photostimulation across the dorsal cortical surface without the constraints imposed by chronically implanted fibers. 

Various approaches have been used to flexibly deliver light to the dorsal cortex without the use of a chronic implant.  An optical fiber can be mounted on a motorized micro-drive (\cite{Zatka-Haas2021SensoryDecision}), a digital micro-mirror device (DMD, \cite{Allen2017ApplicationSignaling}) can project patterns onto the exposed brain to activate extended regions and generate arbitrary illumination patterns (e.g. \cite{Chong2020ManipulatingPerception}), or a pair of galvanometric scan mirrors (galvos) can direct a focused laser beam (\cite{Guo2014FlowMice,Li2016RobustPlanning,Heindorf2018MouseFeedback,Inagaki2018Low-dimensionalCortex,Pinto2019Task-DependentRegions,Keller2020FeedbackCortex,Esmaeili2021RapidResponse,Voitov2022CorticalMemory,Pinto2022MultipleCortex,Coen2023MouseDecisions}). 

The galvo-based option offers the best efficiency-to-utility/affordability trade-off: scanners are much faster than motorized drives, and because the galvos effectively direct all light to the target site, the required laser power is much lower than a DMD. The high speed of the scanners still allows for multiple points to be stimulated within milliseconds, allowing for effective silencing of multiple locations simultaneously. 
Although several groups have implemented galvo-based approaches, there remains no commercial or accessible open source solution for implementation. This is despite the recent widespread adoption of open source hardware tools in neuroscience, such as `Open-Ephys' (\cite{Siegle2017OpenElectrophysiology}), the `Pulse-Pal' pulse generator (\cite{Sanders2014ABehavior}), the `Stimjim' programmable electrical stimulator (\cite{Cermak2019Stimjim:Stimulation}), and numerous light-sheet and multi-photon microscopes. Conversely, implementing random-access photostimulation in head-fixed animals still requires building a system from the ground-up, which is time consuming and requires knowledge of optics, real-time hardware control, and programming.

Here we present `Zapit', an open-source galvo-based photostimulation system. Zapit is the combination of both hardware and software. It features a low-cost design with commercially available components (~7,000 GBP), although a custom-built enclosure can be used to further reduce mechanical noise (~XXXX,000 GBP). We present hardware options for multiple lasers and stimulation wavelengths for use with different opsins. The accompanying software tools facilitate calibration and stimulus delivery and are maintained by a community of developers and experimentalists. We demonstrate how Zapit allows for fast and easy integration of targeted cortical photostimulation in a set of proof of principle behavioral experiments.
