\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Optogenetics is now the principal experimental tool for manipulating neural activity in systems neuroscience due to its high temporal precision, non-invasive options, reversibility, and genetically and spatially restricted specificity (\cite{Boyden2005Millisecond-timescaleActivity,OConnor2009ReverseBrain, Hausser2014Optogenetics:Light, Li2019SpatiotemporalCircuits,Emiliani2022OptogeneticsSystems.}). Optical access to the brain to stimulate optogenetic actuators is predominantly achieved through chronically implanted fibers (e.g. \cite{Mayrhofer2019DistinctTransformation,Babl2019TheInterneurons,Lohse2021SubcorticalMice,Duan2021ATask,Emiliani2022OptogeneticsSystems.}), or implanting a window over the cortical site (e.g. \cite{Schneider2018AMovement,Liu2016Cortico-fugalBehaviour}).
Implanted fibers allow for targeted light delivery in both head-fixed and freely moving behavioral paradigms and enable access to deep brain areas. However, the physical footprint and chronic nature of implants can restrict manipulation to a predetermined and limited number of sites. 
 
In mice, the skull is thin enough ($\sim$250~$\mu m$; \cite{Ghanbari2019Craniobot:Microsurgeries}) to be remarkably transparent (\cite{Steinzeig2017ChronicMice}). This transcranial optical access allows for non-invasive, random access optogenetic photostimulation across the entire dorsal cortical surface. Various approaches exist to achieve this goal. An optical fiber can be mounted on a motorized micro-drive (\cite{Zatka-Haas2021SensoryDecision}), a digital micro-mirror device (DMD, \cite{Allen2017ApplicationSignaling}) can project arbitrary illumination patterns (e.g. \cite{Chong2020ManipulatingPerception}), or a pair of galvanometric scan mirrors (galvos) can direct a focused laser beam to specified positions on the skull (\cite{Guo2014FlowMice,Li2016RobustPlanning,Heindorf2018MouseFeedback,Inagaki2018Low-dimensionalCortex,Pinto2019Task-DependentRegions,Keller2020FeedbackCortex,Esmaeili2021RapidResponse,Voitov2022CorticalMemory,Pinto2022MultipleCortex,Coen2023MouseDecisions,Gauld2025AActions}). 

A galvanometric approach to laser targeting offers the best utility-to-affordability trade-off: scanners are much faster than motorized drives, and because galvo mirrors effectively direct all light to the target site, the required laser power is much lower than a DMD. The high speed of the scanners nonetheless allows the experimenter to silence multiple locations near simultaneously. However, while several groups have implemented galvo-based approaches and provided software (e.g. \cite{Pinto2019Task-DependentRegions}), there remains no commercial or general purpose open-source solution. Polished open-source hardware tools such as  `Open-Ephys' (\cite{Siegle2017OpenElectrophysiology}) and the `Pulse-Pal' pulse generator (\cite{Sanders2014ABehavior}), have transformed experimental neuroscience but no equivalent exists for random-access photostimulation. Implementing random-access photostimulation in head-fixed animals still requires designing a customized system from the ground-up, which is time consuming and requires knowledge of optics, real-time hardware control, and programming.

Here we present `Zapit', an open-source galvo-based photostimulation system that integrates both hardware and software into a user-friendly experimental tool. It features a low-cost design based on commercially available components, with build, usage, and troubleshooting instructions to promote accessibility. The accompanying software tools facilitate semi-automated system calibration, flexible stimulus design and delivery, and are maintained by a community of developers and experimentalists. Through electrophysiological recordings, we confirm that the Zapit system achieves spatiotemporal physiological resolution comparable to existing laser-scanning solutions (\cite {Pinto2022MultipleCortex}, \cite{Guo2014FlowMice}, \cite{Li2019SpatiotemporalCircuits}). Finally, we present proof-of-principle experiments demonstrating that Zapit can evoke robust effects across different sensory and motor systems in awake behaving mice, highlighting the utility and effectiveness of the system in revealing causal links between cortical areas and behavior.
