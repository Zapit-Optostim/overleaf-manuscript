\section{Results}

\subsection{Operating principle}
Zapit is a combined hardware and software solution for galvo-based photostimulation of the mouse dorsal cortex. 
Whilst we provide a compact hardware design (see below), the software is flexible enough to cope with substantial variations to the hardware and can be built entirely from off the shelf parts if desired.
This opens up the technique to research groups who do not have access to a machining workshop.

We implemented a hardware design similar to that used in \citet{Pinto2022MultipleCortex}.
A 470~nm laser is fed into an XY galvo scanner then is pointed down to the animal via a dichroic fold mirror.
A f=100~mm scan lens focuses the beam onto the sample.
This lens doubles as an objective: imaging the sample and any excited green fluorescence onto a camera via a tube lens (Fig.~\ref{fig:operating_principle_01}A).
The galvo waveforms are shaped to minimise opto-mechanical noise, but nonetheless we designed a sealed enclosure for the scanners (Fig.~\ref{fig:operating_principle_01}B).

The Zapit software comprises a user-friendly GUI for performing the two critical alignment steps (Fig.~\ref{fig:operating_principle_01}C). 
\textbf{1.} Aligning the scanners with the camera, enabling the beam to point accurately to any desired location in the image. 
\textbf{2.} Mapping stereotaxic coordinates onto the exposed skull.  
Calibration takes about a minute and, once calibrated, the system will place the beam in any desired location defined by stereotaxic coordinates. 

Once calibrated, a simple API allows the user to integrate stimulation into an experimental paradigm. 
The system supports stimulation of one or more points in a given trial; points are stimulated sequentially. 
In a trial with a single stimulation location, the laser is parked in one place and flashed on and off at 40~Hz with a 50\% duty cycle. 
In a trial with two locations, the beam cycles between them at 40~Hz such that each point experiences a 50\% duty cycle. 
In the case of $\ge$3 points, the beam hits each point at 40~Hz but the duty cycle drops below 50\% and laser power is increased proportionately to maintain the same average power. 
All beam locations are defined in stereotaxic coordinates.
There is a ramp down in intensity at trial end to avoid rebound effects. 
An optional (but highly recommended) blue LED masking light runs in synchrony with the laser.

\subsection{Hardware Design}
For flexible and efficient stimulus control we implemented ScannerMax Saturn 5 scanners into the system, as these perform well and are commercially available (Saturn 5B Dual Axis Galvanometer Scanner; Edmund Optics). 
Images were acquired using a USB-3 Basler acA1920-40um. 
We experimented with a few different lens combinations but eventually settled on an f=100~mm Pl\"{o}ssl objective composed of two ThorLabs AC254-200-A (f=200~mm) achromats.
A Pl\"{o}ssl is a compound lens composed of two identical achromatic doublets arranged such that their positive (convex) surfaces are near touching. 
This arrangement has a focal length half that of one of the individual elements and has substantially reduced optical aberrations and distortions compared with a single achromatic doublet of equivalent focal length. 
The tube lens is an f=50~mm Pl\"{o}ssl composed of two ThorLabs AC254-100-A (f=100~mm) achromats.
Other similar lens combinations also produced good quality images, but we chose this one because it has a magnification of 0.5 and allows the mouse brain to comfortably fit on the camera chip.  
We used a ThorLabs MF252-39 dichroic and MD498 emission filter before the camera. 
A more complete list with suggestions for alternative parts is available \href{https://github.com/Zapit-Optostim/zapit_CAD/blob/main/Parts_List.md}{on GitHub}.
We chose a  473 nm, 75 mW, Coherent Obis laser as the light source. 
Our choice was partly motivated by historical reasons but also because of its reliability and fast ON/OFF times, as well as the unit's relatively high power, which is sufficient deliver up to about 20 sequential points at 40~Hz.

We outsourced design of a custom sealed enclosure for the scanners, optics, and camera to \href{https://www.cosys.org.uk}{COSYS Ltd.}
This box ensured the smallest footprint possible and minimises noise from the scanners. 
A fold mirror placed before the camera keeps the system compact. 
The beam feeds directly into the scan head by mounting the laser at the entry window using a 90 degree cage-mounted fold mirror.
Fibre-coupling is also possible but this would result in light loss, and also increase the NA of the excitation system.
We wanted to avoid this, as the minuscule NA of our current arrangement makes minor focusing errors on the excitation path irrelevant.

We estimated the theoretical laser spot size in Zemax as having a PSF with a FWHM of 70~$\mu m$.
This value is estimated using an f=150~mm objective, slightly longer than the 100~mm we eventually settled on, and a 0.8 mm diameter laser beam.
We measured the PSF full width half max as 91~$\mu m$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:hardware01}).
A larger input beam diameter or a shorter focal length objective would increase excitation NA and so decrease FWHM.

\subsection{Software Design: calibration}
Calibration is a simple two-step procedure. 
First the laser is aligned to the camera image by scanning the beam over a grid of points and conducting an affine transform between the intended and observed beam locations 
This is an automated procedure, initiated by a button-click in a user-friendly GUI (Fig.~\ref{fig:scanner_calib_01}A).

\subsection{Running Experiments in MATLAB}
Once the system is calibrated and a stimulus configuration file is loaded, it becomes possible to integrate photostimulation into an experiment.
This is achieved using Zapit's MATLAB API, which allows presentation of precisely timed photo-stimuli.  
By default the system queues the photo-stimulus with the \texttt{sendSamples} command and waits for the NI DAQ to receive a TTL trigger before presenting it. We confirmed this enables reliable and low latency (\textasciitilde 0.75 ms) presentation of photostimulation (Fig.~\ref{fig:HardwareTriggering_01}A).  Stimuli can also be presented with a fixed delay (Fig.~\ref{fig:HardwareTriggering_01}B) which allows for precise stimulus alignment with different temporal epochs during a behavioural task.
Stimuli can also either run continuously until they are stopped with another software command or they can be defined to run for a precise period of time.
In all cases, laser power is ramped down gradually over a user-defined time period upon stimulation completion (Fig.~\ref{fig:HardwareTriggering_01}).
This avoids large rebounds in activity, which are common following cessation of photoinhibition with light-sensitive opsins.
If an `experiment directory' is defined in Zapit, \texttt{sendSamples} will write a log file there listing each stimulus presentation.

\subsection{Beam Positioning Accuracy}
In trials where $\ge$2 points are stimulated, the beam must be disabled (blanked) whilst it is traveling between locations (Fig.~\ref{fig:beam_pos_01}A).
When the beam is left on, there is the possibility of off-target stimulation (Fig.~\ref{fig:beam_pos_01}B).
We implemented blanking and power control using the built-in modulation facility of our Coherent Obis laser. For simplicity we enforced a fixed motion time of approximately 0.5~ms between any pair of positions. 
Figures~\ref{fig:beam_pos_01}C \& D show that the beam is disabled during this period. 
The scanner position feedback trace from a single trial is shown in blue. 
The laser is pointed at a 1~cm diameter photodiode and the orange trace represents the signal from this sensor. 
When the orange trace is low the laser is off. 
The blanking period of the laser is perfectly timed to correspond with the motion epoch. 
The inset panel in Fig~\ref{fig:beam_pos_01}C shows 100 overlaid trials to demonstrate reliability.

\subsection{Experimental verification of Zapit: Unilateral suppression of ALM biases motor choices during perceptual discrimination}
To verify the functionality of our photostimulation system we set out to replicate a well-known result – biasing directional motor-planning by inactivating anterolateral motor cortex (ALM; \cite{Guo2014FlowMice}). 
First, we trained head-fixed VGAT-ChR2-EYFP mice to discriminate bilateral whisker deflections and report perceptual decisions with directional licking following a short delay (Fig.~\ref{fig:ALM_behav_verification}A). 
On each trial, trains of air-puffs were simultaneously presented to the left and the right whiskers. The target lickport choice (‘left’ or ‘right’) was cued by the whisker stimulus side receiving the faster stimulation rate (freq. range: 0 – 10~Hz). The target lickport dispensed a small water reward for correct choices (3~$\mu l$). Trials where left and right whisker stimulation rate was equal were rewarded at chance. 
The stimulus presentation window (1 s) was followed by a delay (1 s) before mice were allowed to report their choice (Fig.~\ref{fig:ALM_behav_verification}B). 
Following task learning, we programmed Zapit to inactivate left or right ALM while mice performed the task (Fig.~\ref{fig:ALM_behav_verification}C). 
Photoinhibition was delivered on a third of trials and randomised across left and right hemispheres within the same session. 
The optogenetic stimulus was time locked to the onset of the sensory cue and lasted until the beginning of the response window (Fig.~\ref{fig:ALM_behav_verification}B,D). 

On control trials behavioural discrimination showed clear adherence to the signed lateralised difference in whisker-stimulation rate. 
Mice performed with high choice accuracy when the stimulus difference was large and at chance when the stimulation rate was matched bilaterally (Fig.~\ref{fig:ALM_behav_verification}E "Laser OFF"). 
However, behavioural decisions were markedly biased on ALM photoinhibition trials. Unilateral photoinhibition biased choices towards the ipsilateral lickport (Fig.~\ref{fig:ALM_behav_verification}E), consistent with suppression of contralateral motor processing (\cite{Guo2014FlowMice}). 
We then performed additional experiments to characterise the relationship between laser power, unilateral ALM inactivation and behavioural performance. Increasing laser power resulted in incremental decreases in performance on contralateral whisker stimulation trials (Fig.~\ref{fig:ALM_behav_verification}F). 
Our results therefore demonstrate that the Zapit system can be used for flexible, targeted optogenetic manipulations of cortex and can produce reliable and specific effects in awake behaving mice contingent on both perturbation location and stimulus strength.

\subsection{Experimental verification of Zapit: Bilateral multi-site and multi-power stimulation in a visual decision making task}
We next tested the performance of Zapit using bilateral stimulation for multiple sites and multiple light powers in the same session. 
We trained mice on a visual change detection task and tested the effect of stimulating an area expected to influence visual guided behaviour: primary visual cortex (V1), and a control area not expected to influence visual guided behaviour: primary somatosensory cortex (S1). In the same session we stimulated each of these areas 7\% of the trials with either 2 mW or 4 mW laser power. 

Mice were trained to be stationary on a running wheel while observing a drifting grating stimulus, whose speed fluctuated noisily every 50 ms around a geometric mean temporal frequency (TF) of 1Hz (STD = 0.25 octaves), and to report a sustained increase in its speed by licking a central reward spout. 
The mice were motivated to react promptly upon detecting a change by limiting the time in which the reward was accessible to 2.15 seconds . 
Since changes in speed were often ambiguous, their timing unpredictable and the change magnitude randomised, mice had to continuously track the visual stimulus for a prolonged duration (3-15.5 s) prior to the change (Fig.~\ref{fig:Scanning_setup_exp_verification_fig}A).

On laser trials, Zapit began bilateral stimulation 250 ms prior to stimulus onset, and lasted until the end of the stimulus (whether ended by a mouse action, or after the respond window expired), and subsequently ramped down over 250 ms (Fig.~\ref{fig:Scanning_setup_exp_verification_fig}A). 
Throughout, the laser fluctuated according to a bilateral 40 Hz square pulse pattern with a 50\% duty cycle. 
We also presented a blue LED masking light on every trial to avoid mice being able to see the laser on laser trials. 
This masking light had the same stimulation profile (i.e., 40 Hz pulses) as the laser stimulation on opto-stimulation trials.

As expected, we found that silencing V1 created a robust deficit in visual detection of changes in temporal frequency which scaled with increase in light power (Fig.~\ref{fig:Scanning_setup_exp_verification_fig}B,C). At the same time, we did not find that stimulation of S1 had any effect on the mice's ability to detect the visual changes at 2 mW (Fig.~\ref{fig:Scanning_setup_exp_verification_fig}B,C). As shown by the example mouse, clear separation of S1 at 4 mW can be achieved (Fig.~\ref{fig:Scanning_setup_exp_verification_fig}B). However, when stimulating S1 with 4mW laser power, we did see a slight but non-significant flattening of the psychometric curve in the average across mice, possibly indicating that the the light stimulation in some cases may have been affecting higher-order visual areas around 1.5 mm caudal to the stimulation target in S1 when light powers were high (Fig.~\ref{fig:Scanning_setup_exp_verification_fig}C). 

This demonstrates that Zapit can effectively modulate activity at multiple cortical areas at multiple light powers in the same session to directly compare effects of silencing distinct areas bilaterally in the same sessions.
